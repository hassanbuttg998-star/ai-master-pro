How to Write Better AI Image Prompts: A Practical Guide

A simple, repeatable structure for writing AI image prompts: subject, style, lighting, lens, composition and mood, with examples and common mistakes to avoid.

By Hassan Fraz November 2026 7 min read

Two people can type almost the same idea into an AI image generator and get very different results. The difference is rarely luck. It is almost always the prompt. A good prompt gives the model clear, specific instructions about what to draw and how it should look. A weak prompt leaves the model to guess, and the guesses tend to be generic.

This guide explains a simple structure you can reuse for every image, the words that make the biggest difference, and the mistakes that quietly ruin results. Everything here works with the popular image generators such as Midjourney, Stable Diffusion and Leonardo AI, because they all read your text in a similar way.

1. Start with the subject, and be specific

The subject is the main thing in your picture. "A man" is vague. "A 30-year-old man with short black hair and a trimmed beard, wearing a dark green wool suit" is a clear subject. Add details that you would notice if you were describing a photograph to a friend: age, clothing, expression, pose, and what the person or object is doing.

The same applies to objects and scenes. "A car" will give you an average car. "A vintage teal scooter parked outside a small cafe" gives the model something to build on.

2. Say what kind of image it is

Tell the generator the medium. Is it a photograph, a 3D render, an oil painting, a pencil sketch, a flat vector illustration, or an anime frame? This one phrase changes the whole look. If you want something realistic, words such as "professional photograph", "35mm photo" or "studio portrait" help. If you want a stylised result, name the style directly, for example "Pixar-style 3D character" or "Studio Ghibli inspired watercolor".

3. Describe the light

Lighting is the most underused part of a prompt, and it has the biggest effect on mood. A few reliable options:

  • Soft window light for natural, flattering portraits.
  • Golden hour for warm, glowing outdoor scenes.
  • Rim light or backlight for a glowing outline around the subject.
  • Neon lighting for city nights and cyberpunk looks.
  • Hard, directional light for dramatic, high-contrast images.
  • Overcast, diffused light for calm, even, soft images.

4. Add camera and lens language

Even if you are not a photographer, camera terms guide the model toward a believable image. "85mm lens, shallow depth of field" gives a portrait with a softly blurred background. "Wide-angle 24mm" suits landscapes and architecture. "Low angle", "top-down" and "close-up" control the viewpoint. You do not need to be technically exact. The model has learned what these words usually look like.

5. Set the composition and the background

Say where the subject sits in the frame and what is behind it. "Centered composition", "rule of thirds" and "negative space on the right" are useful, especially if you plan to add text later, as you would on a poster. Describe the background briefly: "blurred cobblestone street", "plain light grey studio backdrop", "foggy pine forest".

6. Choose a mood and a color palette

Mood words such as calm, dramatic, mysterious, joyful or moody help the model choose the right details. Color words make the result more consistent: "teal and orange color grading", "muted earth tones", "black and white with high contrast". If you have a brand color, name it.

A simple formula you can copy

Put the parts in this order, separated by commas:

[Subject + details], [medium or style], [lighting], [camera or lens], [composition and background], [mood and color palette], [quality words]

Here is a complete example built from that formula:

A young man in a dark green three-piece suit sitting on a vintage teal scooter, relaxed pose, professional lifestyle photograph, warm golden hour light, 85mm lens with shallow depth of field, blurred old European street cafe in the background, warm earthy color palette, highly detailed, sharp focus.

Every phrase in that prompt has a job. Remove the lighting phrase and the image becomes flatter. Remove the lens phrase and the background loses its soft blur. That is how you learn what each part does: change one thing at a time and compare the results.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Stuffing too many ideas into one prompt. If you ask for five styles at once, the model blends them into a muddle. Choose one main style.
  • Using only vague quality words. "Beautiful, amazing, stunning" says little. Specific details such as light, lens and color say much more.
  • Contradicting yourself. "Bright sunny day" and "dark moody night" in the same prompt confuse the result.
  • Ignoring what you do not want. Many tools have a negative prompt field. Use it for things like "blurry, extra fingers, distorted hands, text, watermark".
  • Changing everything between attempts. If you change five things at once you cannot tell which change helped. Adjust one element per attempt.

Learn from prompts that already work

The fastest way to improve is to study real examples. Every image in the FRAZX PRO galleries is shown together with the exact prompt that produced it, so you can see which words lead to which look. Copy a prompt, change the subject to your own idea, and compare the result with the original. After a few tries you will start to recognise which details matter most for the kind of images you want to make.

If you would rather start from a ready structure, the Cinematic Vision tool expands a short idea into a detailed prompt with camera, lighting and color grading already filled in, and the Portrait Transformer offers tested prompts for styles such as anime, Pixar 3D, oil painting and cyberpunk.

Good prompting is a skill like any other. Start with the structure above, keep notes on what works, and your results will improve quickly.
